Diwali is done, the sweets are almost finished, and the WhatsApp invitations have started arriving. Mehendi on Friday, sangeet on Saturday, baraat and pheras on Sunday night, reception the week after. For most of the family, shaadi season is pure fun. For a child who finds noise, crowds and surprises hard, it can feel like days of chaos with no end in sight.

The good news: you don't have to skip every family wedding. A bit of preparation, a few pictures and a clear exit plan can make a big difference.

## Why weddings are hard for many neurodiverse children

Think about an Indian wedding from your child's point of view:

- **The routine disappears.** Meals are late, bedtime is after midnight, and the day has no familiar shape.
- **The senses get flooded.** A dhol right next to you, DJ speakers, flashing lights, strong perfume, heavy food smells, scratchy embroidered clothes.
- **Lots of people, lots of touching.** Relatives who want a hug, a cheek-pinch, a "Beta, aunty ko namaste karo!"
- **Waiting with no end.** "Baraat aa rahi hai" can mean ten minutes or two hours.

None of this means your child is being "naughty" when they melt down at the sangeet. It usually means they have run out of ways to cope. Our job is to give them more ways, before the day arrives.

## Step 1: Decide which parts you'll attend

You don't need to do everything. Look at the full list of functions and ask honestly: which ones matter most to the family, and which ones can your child manage?

Australia's Raising Children Network suggests going to parties or social events "only for the parts your child can manage" ([Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/autism/school-play-work/social-life/parties-social-events-asd)). For a wedding, that might look like:

- Haldi and mehendi at home in the daytime: **yes**, they are smaller and familiar.
- Sangeet with a DJ till 1 a.m.: **maybe the first hour**, then home.
- Baraat: **watch from a balcony or a quiet spot**, not in the middle of the dhol.
- Pheras late at night: **skip**, and see the photos the next morning.
- Reception: **yes, early**, meet the couple, eat, leave before the crowd peaks.

Agree in advance which adult will leave with your child when needed, so nobody argues about it in the middle of the hall.

## Step 2: Make a wedding-day visual schedule

A visual schedule shows your child what is happening and what comes next, in pictures. The 2020 review by the National Clearinghouse on Autism Evidence and Practice (NCAEP) found that visual supports meet the criteria for an evidence-based practice for autistic children and young people ([AFIRM Visual Supports brief](https://afirm.fpg.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/Visual-Supports-Brief-Packet.pdf)).

For a wedding day, keep it short and concrete. For example:

- **Nahana** (bath)
- **Kapde pehenna** (wear wedding clothes)
- **Car mein jaana** (car ride)
- **Hall pahunchna** (arrive at the hall)
- **Dulha-dulhan ko milna** (meet the bride and groom)
- **Khana khana** (eat dinner)
- **Quiet corner break**
- **Photo** (one family photo)
- **Ghar jaana** (go home)
- **Sona** (sleep)

That last two-card stretch, "Ghar jaana" and "Sona", is the most important part. Many children cope much better when they can *see* that the event ends.

### Tips for using it on the day

- **Carry it with you.** A small printed strip in your bag, or the schedule open on your phone.
- **Move or cross off each card** as it's done, so your child can see progress.
- **Add a "surprise" or "change" card** for the things you can't predict, like the baraat being late. Practise using it at home first.
- **Use first-then for tough moments.** "Pehle photo, phir quiet corner."

## Step 3: Tell the story of the wedding before it happens

A short, simple story about the wedding, read over the week before, can help your child know what to expect. These are often called **social stories** or **social narratives**. The AFIRM team at the University of North Carolina describes social narratives as interventions that "describe social situations in order to highlight relevant features", and notes that, based on the 2020 NCAEP review, they meet the evidence-based practice criteria ([AFIRM Social Narratives brief](https://afirm.fpg.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/Social-Narratives-Brief-Packet-Sam-AFIRM-Team-Updated-2025.pdf)).

Use your child's language and real photos if you can: the hall, the cousin getting married, their outfit. A few lines might read:

*"Riya Didi is getting married. This is called a shaadi. Many people will come. There will be music. Sometimes the music is loud. If it is too loud, I can wear my headphones or go to the quiet corner with Mumma. After dinner, we will go home and sleep in my bed."*

The story names the hard parts honestly, gives your child something they *can* do, and ends somewhere safe and familiar. Read it at bedtime for a few nights.

## Step 4: Plan for the senses

You know your child best, so plan around what bothers them most.

- **Sound:** Noise-cancelling headphones or ear defenders can help a lot. Let your child wear them at home for a few days first so they feel normal, not strange. Stay away from the speakers and the dhol.
- **Clothes:** A sherwani or lehenga can be itchy and heavy. Try the outfit on at home, add a soft cotton layer underneath, or let your child wear something simpler. Comfort matters more than matching the family's colour theme.
- **Food:** Buffets are loud and crowded, and the food may be new. Pack a tiffin of foods your child likes. Raising Children Network suggests food that is "sensory friendly and that your child likes" for social events ([Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/autism/school-play-work/social-life/parties-social-events-asd)).
- **Comfort kit:** A small bag with a favourite toy, a fidget, a snack, water, a spare set of clothes, and the printed schedule.

## Step 5: Find the quiet corner first

When you arrive, before anything else, find a quiet place: a side room, the car, a corridor, a garden outside the hall. Show it to your child: "*Yeh hamara quiet corner hai.*"

Raising Children Network suggests planning what you will do if your child feels overwhelmed, such as taking a walk or sitting in a quiet room ([Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/autism/school-play-work/social-life/parties-social-events-asd)). Breaks work best when they are offered *before* a meltdown, not after. Watch for your child's early signs, like covering ears, pacing, getting very quiet or very loud, and offer the break card early.

## Step 6: Prepare the family too

A short message to close family beforehand can save a lot of stress:

*"Aryan is very excited to come to Riya's wedding. Loud music and crowds can be hard for him, so he may wear headphones and take breaks outside. Please don't worry if he doesn't hug or say namaste. A wave or a high-five is perfect!"*

Ask one trusted cousin or aunt to be your helper on the day: someone who can sit with your child for ten minutes so you can see the jaimala, or who can walk with you to the car if you need to leave.

## Step 7: Plan the day after

Expect your child to need a quiet, predictable day afterwards: a familiar breakfast, their usual schedule, more rest. Go back to your normal visual schedule as soon as you can.

## A gentle reminder

Some weddings will go beautifully. Some will end with you in the car park after twenty minutes. Both are okay. Leaving early is not a failure; it is good parenting. Every time your child gets through part of a big event with support, they learn a little more about what helps them.
