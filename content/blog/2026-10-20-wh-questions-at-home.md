"Beta, what did you eat at school?" Silence. Or maybe the answer is "school". Or the question just comes back to you: "What did you eat?"

If this sounds familiar, you are not alone. Many parents of neurodiverse children tell us WH-questions — *what, where, who, when, why* — are where conversations seem to stop. Your child may know lots of words, name pictures easily, even sing full songs, and still find "Where is your bag?" confusing.

This post is about why that happens and what you can gently practise at home, in the middle of normal family life. No flashcard drills needed.

## Why WH-questions are tricky

A WH-question asks your child to do a few things at once. They have to notice the question word, understand what *kind* of answer it wants (a thing, a place, a person, a time), search their memory, and then put the answer into words — all while someone is waiting.

Some question words are also more abstract than others. "What" and "where" are usually about things you can see and point to. "When" and "why" are about time and reasons, which you can't point at. So it often makes sense to start with the concrete ones and build up slowly.

Every child is different, and your child's speech therapist knows your child best. If you have one, ask them which question words to focus on first. The ideas below are general home-practice ideas, not a therapy programme.

## Start with what your child can see

The easiest questions are about things that are right in front of you. That way your child doesn't have to remember anything — the answer is visible.

- **What:** Hold up a banana at snack time. "What is this?" — "Kela!" or "Banana!" Both are right.
- **Where:** While getting ready, "Where are your shoes?" and let them point or go and fetch them. Pointing is a real answer.
- **Who:** Look at a family photo together. "Who is this?" — "Nani!"

Accept any language your child uses. A bilingual home is a strength, not a problem. If your child answers a Hindi question in English, or mixes both ("Mumma, *joota* there!"), celebrate it.

## Give a choice before you expect a full answer

When an open question feels too big, turn it into a small choice:

- Instead of "What do you want to drink?" try "Doodh or paani?"
- Instead of "Where should we go?" try "Park or Dadi's house?"
- Instead of "Who will help you?" try "Papa or Didi?"

Choices give your child the words they need inside the question. Over time you can offer the open question first, wait, and only offer the choice if they need it.

## Use pictures to show what kind of answer fits

Many children find it easier when the question word has a picture of its own. Some families use simple cues like these:

- **What** — a picture of an object
- **Where** — a picture of a place, like a house or a park
- **Who** — a picture of a face
- **When** — a picture of a clock or the sun and moon

When you ask a question, point to the matching picture. It quietly tells your child, "I'm asking about a *place*," or "I'm asking about a *person*." You can make these cues yourself, print them, or add them next to your child's daily routine.

## Try this today: the "three questions" walk

Here is one small thing you can try today, free, without buying anything.

Next time you go for a short walk, a trip to the sabziwala, or even just to the building gate, pick **three questions** to ask on the way. Only three. For example:

1. "What is that?" (pointing to a dog, a bus, a flower)
2. "Where are we going?" (you can answer first if needed: "We are going to the shop!")
3. "Who is that?" (the watchman uncle, a neighbour)

Ask each question once, then wait. Count slowly to five in your head before you help. That pause is the hardest part for us parents, and it often makes the biggest difference — it gives your child time to think.

If they don't answer, give the answer yourself in a happy voice, and move on. No pressure, no repeating the question five times. Tomorrow, try the same three again. Repetition in a familiar place is how these questions start to feel easy.

## When answers don't come yet

Some days your child will answer everything. Some days nothing. That is normal.

A few things that may help:

- **Model the answer.** Ask yourself the question out loud and answer it: "Where is my phone? My phone is on the table!"
- **Keep the question short.** "Where is ball?" is easier to follow than "Can you tell me where you kept the ball you were playing with?"
- **Notice echoing.** If your child repeats your question back, they may be telling you they're not sure what you want. Try a choice, or show the answer, instead.
- **Go slow with "why".** "Why" questions need a lot of reasoning. It is fine to keep them for later, or to answer them together.

If you're worried about your child's understanding or speech, please talk to your child's speech therapist or paediatrician. They can look at the whole picture.
