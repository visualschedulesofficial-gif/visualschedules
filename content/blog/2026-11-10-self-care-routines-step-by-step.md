It is 7:40 in the morning. The school van comes at 8. Your child is standing at the basin with a toothbrush in one hand, looking at you. You know that if you just do it yourself, it will take two minutes. If you wait for them, it could take ten, and there might be tears.

So most days, you do it yourself. And then a small voice inside asks: *when will they learn to do this on their own?*

If this sounds familiar, you are not alone. Brushing, dressing, washing hands and putting on shoes look like simple tasks to adults. For many neurodiverse children, each one is actually a long chain of small steps, and it is easy to get lost somewhere in the middle.

This post shares simple ways to practise these everyday routines at home. Every child is different, so if your child sees an occupational therapist, please talk to them about which ideas suit your child best. These are home-practice ideas, not a therapy plan.

## Why "go and brush your teeth" can feel so big

When we say "brush karo", we are really asking for ten or more things: go to the bathroom, pick up the brush, open the paste, squeeze a little, close the cap, brush the top teeth, the bottom teeth, the inside, spit, rinse, put everything back.

For a child who finds it hard to plan steps in order, remember what comes next, or cope with the feel of the brush and the taste of the paste, that one instruction can feel like a mountain. They may freeze, wander off, or do step one again and again. It is not stubbornness. They just don't yet have a clear map of the task.

The good news: you can draw that map for them.

## Break it into small, clear steps

Pick **one** routine to start with, ideally one that happens every day at a calm time. Handwashing before lunch is often a good first choice because it is short.

Then write down every step, the way you actually do it. For handwashing, it might be:

- Tap on
- Hands wet
- Soap lo
- Rub, rub, rub (front, back, fingers)
- Rinse
- Tap off
- Towel se pocho

Keep the words short and the same every time. If your child understands Hindi better, use Hindi. If English works, use English. Many families mix both, and that is perfectly fine. What matters is that "soap lo" means the same thing on Monday and on Saturday.

## Show, don't just say

Spoken instructions disappear the moment they are said. A picture stays.

Put a small strip of pictures, one picture per step, right where the routine happens: next to the basin, inside the cupboard door, near the shoe rack. Your child can look at it as many times as they need without anyone repeating themselves.

You can draw simple pictures, take photos of your child doing each step, or make a printable sequence in [Visual Schedules](https://visualschedule.app), which has bilingual Hindi-English cards and both girl and boy characters. Whatever you use, keep it at your child's eye level.

## Help less, a little at a time

At first, you may need to guide your child's hands through most steps. That is okay. The aim is to slowly step back.

A helpful way to think about it is from more help to less help:

- **Hand-over-hand:** your hands gently guide theirs
- **A light touch:** a tap on the elbow to start the next step
- **Pointing:** you point to the picture or the soap
- **Just waiting:** you stand nearby and let them try

Move down this list slowly. If your child gets upset or stuck, go back one level for a few days. Progress is rarely a straight line, and some days will be harder than others. That is part of learning, not a sign of failure.

## Try this today: start from the end

Here is one small idea you can try tonight. It is called **backward chaining**, and many therapists use it.

Instead of teaching the first step first, you do all the steps *with* your child, except the very last one. Let them do that last step on their own.

So with handwashing, you help with tap, water, soap, rubbing and rinsing. Then you say, "Ab tum. Towel se pocho," and let them dry their hands by themselves.

Then celebrate: "Wah! Tumne khud kiya!"

Why start at the end? Because your child gets to *finish* the task and feel that "done!" moment every single time. Once the last step is easy, they do the last two steps. Then the last three. Slowly, the whole routine becomes theirs.

A few small tips:

- Practise at a calm time, not when you are rushing for the school van
- Keep it short. One routine, a few minutes
- Praise the effort, not just the result
- Notice sensory needs. If the water is too cold or the towel too rough, a small change can make a big difference

## When routines are part of the whole day

Self-care steps fit inside a bigger picture: the day itself. A child who knows "first brush, then breakfast, then school van" often finds each small routine easier, because they know where it sits and what comes after.

That is where a simple daily [visual schedule](https://visualschedule.app) can help, with the big routine on one strip and the small steps for each task on another. If you have read our earlier post on [first-then boards](https://visualschedule.app/blog/first-then-board-guide), you will notice the same idea: show your child what is happening, in order, so there are fewer surprises.

## A gentle reminder

Your child will not learn to brush their teeth in a week, and that is okay. Every time they do one step on their own is a real step forward, even if it doesn't look like much to anyone else.

Start with one routine. Break it into small pieces. Put up a picture. Help a little less each week. And on the hard mornings, when you end up doing it yourself so the van doesn't leave, that's okay too. Tomorrow is another chance to try.
