"How was the birthday party?" — "Cake."

That one word might be all you get. And yet you know your child *noticed* things: the balloons, the loud music, the cousin who cried when the candles were blown out. The memories are there. Putting them in order and turning them into a little story is the hard part.

Telling a story — even a three-sentence one — is a big skill. It is how children share their day, ask for help, explain what went wrong at school, and later, make friends. For many neurodiverse children, this skill needs a little extra support and a lot of practice in a calm, low-pressure way.

This post shares simple things you can practise at home, during normal family life. Every child is different, so if your child sees a speech therapist, talk to them about which ideas suit your child best. These are home-practice ideas, not a therapy programme.

## Why storytelling feels hard

When we tell a story, we are doing many things at once without noticing. We remember what happened, pick the important parts, put them in order, choose words like "first", "then" and "after that", and keep our listener in mind.

That's a lot to juggle. A child might:

- Know all the words but not the order they go in
- Jump straight to the end ("Then we came home")
- Talk about one detail they loved, again and again
- Freeze when asked an open question like "Tell me about your day"

None of this means your child "can't" tell stories. It usually means the task is too big right now, and we can make it smaller.

## Start with stories that are already in front of you

The easiest stories are ones your child can see while telling them. That way, memory isn't doing all the work.

- **Picture books:** Choose books with clear pictures and little text. Instead of reading every word, point and narrate: "Look, the *billi* is hungry. She is looking for milk."
- **Family photos:** Scroll through photos from a trip to Nani's house. "Here we are at the station. Then we got on the train. Then — look! — Nani at the door."
- **Your phone's videos:** A short clip of your child at the park is a ready-made story with a beginning, middle and end.

At first, *you* tell the story and your child listens, points or fills in one word. That counts. Listening to many small stories is how children learn what a story sounds like.

## Use "first, then, last" as your story frame

Most simple stories can be told in three steps. This small frame gives your child a structure to hang their words on.

- **Pehle** (first): "First we went to the market."
- **Phir** (then): "Then we bought *aloo* and *tamatar*."
- **Aakhir mein** (last): "Last, we came home and made *sabzi*."

You can draw three boxes on a piece of paper, or use three picture cards in a row, and point to each one as you go. If your child already uses a first-then board or a visual schedule, this will feel familiar — a story is a bit like a schedule of something that already happened.

On visualschedule.app you can put a few picture cards in order for any everyday activity. Once the day is done, the same cards can become a story: "We did this, then this, then this."

## Turn everyday moments into tiny stories

You don't need special story time. Small stories are everywhere:

- **After bath:** "First water, then soap, then towel! Now you're clean."
- **After cooking together:** "What did we make? We mixed the *atta*, then rolled it, then Mumma put it on the *tawa*."
- **After a tantrum, once everyone is calm:** "The tower fell down. You felt sad. Then we built it again." Keep this one gentle and short — it helps your child make sense of big feelings without blame.

Speak in whichever language feels natural at home. If your child mixes Hindi and English in one sentence — "Then we ate *jalebi*!" — that's a complete, lovely story. A bilingual home is a strength.

## Make it easier before you make it harder

If an open question like "What happened at school?" gets no answer, try a few steps down the ladder:

- **Give a choice:** "Did you play with blocks or with sand?"
- **Start the sentence for them:** "At school, I played with…"
- **Use a picture:** Show the class photo or the drawing they brought home and ask about that.

Once your child is comfortable at one step, wait a little longer before you help. A few seconds of quiet is often all a child needs to find their words.

## Keep it playful, not a test

Stories should feel like connection, not homework. A few ways to keep it light:

- Act out stories with toys — a teddy who goes to the doctor, a car that gets stuck in traffic.
- Let your child be silly. If the elephant flies to Mumbai in a rickshaw, wonderful.
- Swap roles: you get it "wrong" ("First we ate dinner, then we cooked it!") and let your child correct you. Children love fixing a parent's mistakes.
- Repeat favourite stories. Hearing the same story many times is not boring for most children — it is how they learn it well enough to tell it themselves.

## One small tip to try today

Tonight, before bed, try a **"three-picture day"**. Pick three photos from your phone of things your child did today — breakfast, the park, bath time. Show them one at a time and tell the story together:

"First, we ate *poha*. Then we went to the park. Last, we had a bath."

On the first night, you can tell the whole thing yourself. After a few nights, pause before the last word and let your child fill it in: "Then we went to the…" — "Park!" Slowly, over days or weeks, you can leave more of the story for them.

It takes two minutes, needs nothing you don't already have, and ends the day on a warm, shared memory.
