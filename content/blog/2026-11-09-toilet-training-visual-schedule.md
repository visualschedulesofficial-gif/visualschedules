"Beta, susu aayi?" If you have asked this question fifty times today and still ended up washing another pair of shorts, you are not alone. Toilet training is one of the most common worries for parents of neurodiverse children, especially with school admissions and relatives' comments adding pressure.

Here is the good news: many autistic and neurodiverse children learn toilet skills well when the steps are **shown**, not just **said**. A simple visual schedule, a fixed routine and a calm bathroom can turn toilet time from a daily battle into something your child understands and can predict.

## First, is your child ready?

Autistic children usually show the same readiness signs as other children, though sometimes later. Australia's Raising Children Network lists signs such as ([Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/autism/health-wellbeing/toileting-hygiene/toilet-training-autism)):

- Your child lets you know, with words, a sign or a gesture, that they have had an accident
- They can follow a simple instruction like "toilet mein baitho"
- They can pull their pants up and down, or are learning to
- Their bowel movements are fairly regular
- They stay dry for about two hours during the day

The same page suggests checking with your child's paediatrician **before** you start, to rule out problems like constipation. If anything feels off, talk to your child's doctor or therapist first.

## Why pictures work better than reminders

Toileting looks like one task to us. For a child, it is a long chain: notice the feeling, go to the bathroom, open the door, pull down pants, sit, do susu or potty, wash, pull up pants, flush, wash hands, dry hands.

When we only use words ("Chalo, jaldi karo, haath dho!"), the child has to hold all of that in memory while also coping with the sounds, smells and feelings of the bathroom.

The UK's National Autistic Society recommends putting a visual sequence **beside the toilet**, using photos, pictures or words, whichever your child responds to best. They also suggest using visual sequences rather than spoken prompts, because children can become dependent on verbal reminders ([National Autistic Society](https://autism.org.uk/about/health/toilet-training.aspx)). In other words, the picture becomes the teacher, so you don't have to keep repeating yourself.

## Step 1: Make a toilet visual schedule

Keep it short and clear. For a young child, start with 4 to 6 pictures, for example:

- **Pants down** – Pant neeche
- **Sit on toilet** – Toilet par baitho
- **Susu / potty** – Susu karo
- **Wash** – Paani se saaf karo
- **Pants up** – Pant upar
- **Wash hands** – Haath dho lo

A few tips that make a big difference:

- **Show exactly what you're teaching.** If your son is learning to sit, show a picture of sitting, not standing ([National Autistic Society](https://autism.org.uk/about/health/toilet-training.aspx)).
- **Match your bathroom.** Many Indian homes use a health faucet, a mug and bucket, or an Indian-style toilet. Your pictures should show what your child actually uses at home, not a bathroom from a foreign picture book.
- **Use one word for the toilet.** Pick "toilet", "susu" or "bathroom" and have everyone in the house use that same word. Raising Children Network also recommends one consistent word across the household.
- **Stick it at eye level.** One strip next to the toilet, and a short handwashing strip above the sink.

## Step 2: Teach the schedule outside the bathroom first

Don't wait until your child is bursting to introduce the pictures. Sit together at a calm time and go through the strip like a little story: "Pehle pant neeche, phir baitho, phir susu..."

Raising Children Network suggests going over the schedule with your child two or three times a day. You can act it out with a doll or teddy, or let your child point to each picture as you name it.

## Step 3: Set fixed toilet times

Instead of waiting for your child to ask, build toilet visits into the day at the same times. This is sometimes called **habit training**. A simple starting routine might be:

- After waking up
- After breakfast and after lunch
- Before going out
- After coming home from school or therapy
- Before bath
- Before bed

Watch for patterns too. If accidents usually happen around 11 a.m., take your child just before that time. The National Autistic Society suggests noticing when your child usually does susu or potty and using those times for toilet visits.

Keep each sit short. Start with a few seconds and build up slowly, to no more than about 5 minutes, so toilet time never feels like a punishment ([Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/autism/health-wellbeing/toileting-hygiene/toilet-training-autism)). Follow the whole picture sequence even if nothing happens. The routine itself is what your child is learning.

## Step 4: Make the bathroom a calmer place

For many neurodiverse children, the bathroom is a sensory challenge: echoing tiles, a loud flush, cold water, strong phenyl smell, bright tube lights, a geyser that clicks and hums.

Small changes can help:

- **Footstool under the feet.** Feeling steady makes sitting much easier, especially on a western toilet.
- **Child seat on the toilet.** If change is hard for your child, a training seat on the regular toilet can be easier than moving from a potty later.
- **Flush at the end, or later.** If the flush sound is scary, let your child step out first and slowly build up to watching from the doorway ([National Autistic Society](https://autism.org.uk/about/health/toilet-training.aspx)).
- **Comfort object.** Let them hold a favourite toy while sitting.
- **Mild soap, lukewarm water** and soft lighting where possible.

## Step 5: Celebrate the small wins

Praise exactly what your child did: "Wah! Tumne khud pant neeche kiya!" That is more useful than a general "good boy" or "good girl".

You can also use a first-then board: **First toilet, then bubbles** (or a favourite song, or two minutes of a toy). If you haven't tried one yet, our guide to [first-then boards](https://visualschedule.app/blog/first-then-board-guide) explains how to start.

Decide in advance what earns a reward, keep it small and immediate, and slowly fade the rewards once the skill is steady, while keeping the praise going.

## Step 6: Get everyone on the same page

Toilet training often slows down when every adult does it differently. Dadi says "potty", the school says "washroom", the didi at home rushes through the steps.

Give a copy of the same visual schedule to:

- Grandparents and other caregivers at home
- Your child's school or daycare
- Your child's therapist, so they can practise it in sessions too

Both Raising Children Network and the National Autistic Society stress that consistency across everyone who helps your child matters a lot.

## When things get hard

Accidents are part of learning. Clean up calmly, without scolding, and move on. Some days will feel like a step backward, especially during illness, travel, festivals or a change in school.

Please talk to your child's doctor or therapist if:

- Your child seems to hold in or avoid bowel movements
- They show strong fear of the toilet or distress you can't settle
- Progress stops or goes backward for a long time

And if toilet training becomes a daily fight, it is okay to pause and try again later. Raising Children Network suggests a break of about three months, and says plainly: "Don't feel that you've failed."

You don't need to do everything at once. This week, try just three things:

- Make a 4 to 6 step toilet strip and stick it next to the toilet
- Go through it with your child twice a day, outside the bathroom
