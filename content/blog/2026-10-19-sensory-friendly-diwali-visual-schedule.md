Diwali is one of the most beautiful times of the year. Diyas on the balcony, rangoli at the door, the smell of ghee and besan from the kitchen, cousins arriving, new clothes. For many of our children, though, it is also a lot. Five days of changed routines, loud crackers late into the night, flashing lights, crowded homes and relatives asking "Beta, naam batao?"

This year Diwali (Lakshmi Puja) falls on **Sunday, 8 November 2026** ([source](https://www.smartpuja.com/blog/diwali-2026-date-lakshmi-puja-muhurat-vidhi/)). That gives you a little under three weeks to prepare. The good news: a few small changes, planned in advance, can make the festival far more enjoyable for your child and for you.

Here is a practical plan, built around one simple tool: a **visual schedule**.

## Why a visual schedule helps during festivals

Festivals take away the predictability many neurodiverse children rely on. School is off, meals shift, bedtime moves, and the house fills with people. When a child doesn't know what is coming next, worry often comes out as meltdowns, shutdowns or refusing to leave the room.

A visual schedule makes the day *seeable*. Instead of holding a string of spoken instructions in their head, your child can look at pictures and know: first bath, then new clothes, then puja, then dinner, then quiet time.

Visual supports are not just a nice idea. The 2020 review by the National Clearinghouse on Autism Evidence and Practice (NCAEP) lists visual supports as an evidence-based practice for autistic children and young people ([Texas Statewide Leadership for Autism Training](https://www.txautism.net/interventions/visual-supports); [AFIRM Visual Supports brief](https://afirm.fpg.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/Visual-Supports-Brief-Packet.pdf)). Every child is different, so do talk to your child's therapist about what suits them best.

## Step 1: Show the "big picture" of the five days

Before you plan each day, help your child see that Diwali is a *few special days*, with a beginning and an end. Make a simple strip with one card for each day:

- **Dhanteras** – shopping / new utensils
- **Choti Diwali** – rangoli, diyas, maybe a visit
- **Badi Diwali / Lakshmi Puja** – puja, sweets, crackers outside
- **Govardhan Puja / Padwa** – family lunch
- **Bhai Dooj** – tilak with bhaiya or didi

Your family's traditions may look different, and that's fine. Use the days *you* celebrate. Put a big tick or move a card to the "done" side each night, so your child can see that the festival is moving along and normal days are coming back. A line like *"Paanch din Diwali, phir school"* (five days of Diwali, then school) can be surprisingly reassuring.

## Step 2: Make a daily schedule for the busiest day

Lakshmi Puja day is usually the most intense, so give it its own detailed schedule. For example:

- Wake up – *uthna*
- Bath with ubtan (or a regular bath, if ubtan feels too sticky)
- New clothes – *naye kapde*
- Help make rangoli
- Lunch
- **Quiet time** in the bedroom
- Light diyas with Mumma
- Puja – *aarti* and *prasad*
- Dinner
- Crackers (watch from the window, or go down with headphones)
- Sleep – *sona*

Keep the schedule short enough to follow. For younger children, three or four cards at a time is often plenty. Add the full list on the fridge and a small "now and next" version for the moment.

## Step 3: Use first-then for the hard parts

Some parts of Diwali may simply not be fun for your child: wearing a stiff kurta, sitting through the full aarti, greeting a room full of relatives. A **first-then board** keeps it simple and honest:

- *Pehle naye kapde, phir tablet.* (First new clothes, then tablet.)
- *Pehle aarti, phir laddoo.* (First aarti, then laddoo.)
- *Pehle Nani ko namaste, phir apne room mein.* (First namaste to Nani, then to your room.)

Keep the "then" something your child truly wants, and keep the "first" small and achievable. You can always shorten the task. Two minutes of aarti is still taking part.

## Step 4: Prepare for crackers

For many children, the sound of crackers is the hardest part of Diwali, and it can go on for hours. The National Autistic Society's advice for fireworks nights in the UK works very well here too ([National Autistic Society](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/going-out-leisure-and-travel/bonfire-night/all-audiences)):

- **Talk about it beforehand.** Show short videos of diyas, phuljhadi (sparklers) and crackers at low volume, so the sounds are not a surprise. Add a "crackers" card to the schedule so your child knows *when* to expect them.
- **Ear defenders or headphones.** Let your child wear them around the house for a few days *before* Diwali so they feel familiar, not strange.
- **A familiar distraction.** A favourite song, show or game can help cover the noise and flashes.
- **A safe quiet space.** Pick one room, ideally the one furthest from the road, with curtains drawn. Put a "quiet room" card on the schedule so your child knows they can go there any time.
- **Comfort items.** Favourite snacks, a soft blanket or a cuddly toy can help them settle.
- **Involve your child.** Every child is different. Let them choose: watch from the window, go down for five minutes, or stay inside. All of these are fine.

If crackers continue near bedtime, add a calming bedtime routine to the schedule (bath, pyjamas, story, lights off) with headphones or soft music, and expect a slightly later night.

## Step 5: Plan for guests and visits

Relatives mean well, but questions, hugs and cheek-pinching can overwhelm a child quickly. A few ideas:

- **Make a "who is coming" card** with photos of the guests. *"Aaj Mausi aur Chachu aayenge."*
- **Agree on a greeting your child is comfortable with**: namaste, a wave, a high-five, or just being in the room. Let family know in advance, gently.
- **If you're visiting someone**, add a "car – Nani's house – home" sequence to the schedule, and pack a small bag with headphones, a snack and a familiar toy.
- **Give yourself permission to leave early.** A short, happy visit is better than a long, difficult one.

## Step 6: Keep some things the same

Not everything has to change. Try to protect a few anchors from your child's regular day: the same breakfast, the usual bath time, a favourite bedtime story. These familiar pieces make the new ones easier to handle.

Sensory-wise, think about clothes too. Many festive outfits are scratchy or tight. Cotton kurtas, cutting off tags, or letting your child change into comfortable clothes after puja photos can prevent a lot of distress.

## After Diwali: back to routine

The days after the festival matter as much as the festival itself. Go back to the regular school-day schedule as soon as you can, and show it to your child the night before: *"Kal se school wala din."* Expect a day or two of tiredness or extra sensitivity. That is very common after a big change.

## A gentle reminder for you

You don't need a perfect Diwali. If your child enjoys one diya, one laddoo and ten calm minutes on the balcony, that is a real celebration. Plan what you can, keep it flexible, and notice what works so next year is even easier.
