December is almost here. School breaks begin, Nani's house is calling, and suddenly the family WhatsApp group is full of train tickets and flight plans. For many parents of neurodiverse children, this is exciting *and* worrying at the same time. "Train mein sambhal paayega?" "What if there's a meltdown at the airport?"

Travel takes away almost everything that helps our children feel safe: the same bed, the same food, the same order of the day. The good news is that we can bring some of that predictability with us. A simple travel visual schedule, a bit of practice and a few smart packing choices can make the journey much calmer for everyone.

## Why travel feels so big for our children

At home, your child knows what comes next. On a trip, there are new places, loud announcements, crowds, waiting in queues and long stretches of sitting still. Many autistic and neurodiverse children find the **unknown** harder than the journey itself.

That is why preparation matters so much. Children's hospitals and autism organisations commonly suggest the same few things: explain what will happen using pictures or short stories, rehearse where you can, plan breaks, and carry comfort items ([Lurie Children's Hospital](https://www.luriechildrens.org/en/blog/travel-tips-for-children-with-autism-how-to-prepare-for-air-travel/); [Autism Spectrum Australia](https://www.aspect.org.au/uploads/documents/Information-Sheets/Preparing-for-travel-info-sheet_Nov25.pdf)). None of this needs to be fancy. A few pictures and a plan go a long way.

## Step 1: Make a "trip story" a week before

Start talking about the trip 5–7 days before you leave, not the night before. Sit with your child and look at pictures of each part of the journey:

- Our house → auto or cab
- Railway station or airport
- Waiting
- Train or plane
- Eating snacks
- Sleeping (for overnight trains)
- Nani's house

Keep the words short and calm: *"Pehle hum station jaayenge. Phir train mein baithenge. Phir Nani ke ghar."* Read this little story once or twice a day. If your child likes videos, look at a short clip of a train or plane together so the sounds are not a surprise.

If your child already uses a [first-then board](https://visualschedule.app/blog/first-then-board-guide), use it during the trip too: *"First security check, then biscuit."*

## Step 2: Build a travel visual schedule

A travel day has many steps, so break it into small pieces your child can see. With [Visual Schedules](https://visualschedule.app), you can make a picture schedule in Hindi-English with a girl or boy character, and print it or keep it on your phone.

A sample schedule for a train journey:

- Wake up and brush teeth
- Breakfast
- Put on shoes
- Cab to the station
- Wait on the platform
- Find our seat in the train
- Snack time
- Play with tablet / colouring
- Toilet break
- Lunch
- Rest
- Get off the train
- Hug Nani!

For a flight, add steps like *check-in*, *security check*, *walk to the gate*, *seatbelt on*, and *plane goes up*. Lurie Children's suggests including the moments when devices must be put away and when they can come back, because those are common trigger points ([Lurie Children's Hospital](https://www.luriechildrens.org/en/blog/travel-tips-for-children-with-autism-how-to-prepare-for-air-travel/)).

**Tip:** Carry a small printed strip with velcro or a clip, so your child can move a "done" picture or tick each step off. Seeing the list get shorter helps with "Kitna time aur?"

## Step 3: Practise the tricky parts at home

Think about which moments are hardest for your child and play them out at home like a game:

- **Waiting in a line**: stand in a "queue" in the hallway and count to 20 together.
- **Sitting with a seatbelt**: practise in the car or on a chair with a dupatta as a "belt".
- **Security check**: pretend to walk through a doorway "machine" and put a bag on the table.
- **Loud announcements**: play a station or airport announcement video at low volume, then a little louder.
- **Using a new toilet**: Indian trains and public toilets can be loud and unfamiliar. If you have been working on [toilet training with a visual schedule](https://visualschedule.app/blog/toilet-training-visual-schedule), carry that same picture sequence with you.

Short, happy practice sessions work better than one long one. Stop while your child is still enjoying it.

## Step 4: Pack a "calm bag"

Keep one small bag that is only for your child and easy to reach. Ideas:

- Noise-cancelling headphones or earmuffs
- A favourite soft toy, blanket or chewy
- Familiar snacks from home (new places often mean new food)
- A water bottle they already use
- A few small new toys or stickers to bring out one at a time
- A charged tablet with downloaded videos, as the network may drop on the way
- A spare set of clothes and a plastic bag
- Their printed visual schedule and first-then board

Also carry an **"About me" card** with your child's name, your phone number, and two or three lines on how to talk to them (for example, *"I don't speak much. Please use short words. I like trains."*). Lurie Children's recommends carrying this kind of information in case your child gets separated from you ([Lurie Children's Hospital](https://www.luriechildrens.org/en/blog/travel-tips-for-children-with-autism-how-to-prepare-for-air-travel/)).

## Step 5: Ask for help at the airport

Many families don't know this: several Indian airports are now part of the **Hidden Disabilities Sunflower** programme. You can collect a sunflower lanyard, badge or wristband at the help desk or information counter. Staff are trained to recognise it and offer help discreetly, for example with queues or extra time.

Delhi Airport is Sunflower-friendly and gives out lanyards, pin badges and wristbands at its help desks and information counters ([Hidden Disabilities Sunflower](https://hdsunflower.com/insights/post/delhi-airport)). Bengaluru's Kempegowda International Airport has also joined ([Hidden Disabilities Sunflower](https://hdsunflower.com/insights/post/kempegowda-international-airport-becomes-sunflower-friendly)), and in February 2026 Thiruvananthapuram airport launched its own Sunflower project; that report also mentions Hyderabad ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/travel/travel-news/2026/02/12/thiruvananthapuram-airport-autism-assistance.html)).

Services change, so **call or check your airport's and airline's website a few days before you fly**. When you book, you can also ask the airline about seating near the front or near the toilet, and tell the crew at boarding that your child may need a little extra time.

## Step 6: On the travel day

- **Keep the morning routine the same** as far as possible: same breakfast, same order.
- **Reach early** so you are not rushing. Rushing parents make children anxious.
- **Use the schedule out loud**: *"Security ho gaya. Ab gate pe jaayenge."*
- **Plan breaks**: a walk down the train corridor, a quiet corner at the gate, a stretch at the station.
- **Watch for early signs** of tiredness or overload, like covering ears, pacing or getting very quiet, and step back before it becomes a meltdown.
- **Offer choices**: *"Biscuit ya chips?"* *"Window ya colouring?"* Small choices give back a sense of control.

If a meltdown does happen, that is okay. It is not bad parenting and it is not "naughty" behaviour. Move to a quieter spot if you can, speak less, offer the comfort item and wait. People around you may stare; your child needs you more than they do.

## Step 7: At Nani's house (or the hotel)

Once you arrive, the routine still matters. Put up a simple daily schedule on the wall or fridge: wake up, breakfast, outing, lunch, rest, play, dinner, sleep. Keep bedtime steps the same as at home, with the same pyjamas, the same story and the same night light.

It also helps to gently prepare relatives. A short message like *"Aarav needs a quiet room for rest after lunch, and he may not want hugs straight away"* saves a lot of awkward moments. ## Coming home is a transition too

Many parents notice that children find the return journey and the first few days back hard. Show the "going home" schedule a day before you leave, and keep the first day at home quiet and familiar before school starts again.

## A small plan makes a big difference

You don't need a perfect trip. You need a few pictures, a calm bag and a plan your child can see. Start with one schedule for travel day, and build from there.
