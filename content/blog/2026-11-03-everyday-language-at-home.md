"Paani."

Your child says one word, you hand over the glass, and the moment is over. It works. Your child got what they needed, and you understood. But somewhere in your mind, a small question sits: *how do we get from one word to more?*

Many parents of neurodiverse children carry this question every day. Maybe your child has a handful of words. Maybe they have lots of words but use them mostly to name things, not to ask, share or tell. Maybe they repeat lines from cartoons but find it hard to say what *they* want.

This post shares simple things you can practise at home, inside normal family life: meals, bath time, getting dressed, going to the market. Every child is different, so if your child sees a speech therapist, please talk to them about which ideas suit your child. These are home-practice ideas, not a therapy programme.

## Why "just talk more" doesn't always help

Well-meaning relatives often say, "Bas usse zyada baat karo, sab theek ho jayega." Talking to your child matters, of course. But for many children, a constant stream of words is hard to follow. Long sentences, lots of questions and a busy room can make it harder, not easier, to pick out the one word they could learn.

What tends to help more is *less, but better*: short phrases, said clearly, at the moment your child is already interested.

## Five small habits that make a big difference

### 1. Follow what your child is looking at

Language sticks best when it is attached to something your child already cares about. If they are staring at the spinning fan, that is your moment: "Fan. Fan ghoom raha hai. Round and round."

You don't need to bring out flashcards. The world around your child is the lesson.

### 2. Add one word

If your child uses one word, you say two. If they use two, you say three. This is sometimes called "one step ahead".

- Child: "Ball." You: "Red ball." or "Ball do."
- Child: "Doodh." You: "Garam doodh." or "More doodh."
- Child: "Car go." You: "Car goes fast!"

You are not asking them to repeat it. You are simply showing what the next step sounds like, again and again, without pressure.

### 3. Pause and wait (longer than feels comfortable)

This is the hardest one for most of us. We ask, "Kya chahiye? Biscuit ya banana?" and then, before our child has had a chance, we answer for them.

Try counting slowly to five in your head after you ask or offer something. Look at your child expectantly and warmly. Many children need that extra time to plan what to say. If nothing comes, that's okay; model the word and give it to them anyway. The waiting is the practice.

### 4. Offer choices instead of yes/no questions

"Do you want juice?" can be answered with a nod. "Juice or milk?" invites a word. Holding up the two real objects helps even more.

Choices work at every level. A child with few words might point or say one word. A child with more language might say, "I want milk, please." Either is a win.

### 5. Use the language your family really speaks

Many Indian homes mix Hindi and English (and often another language too) in the same sentence. That is real life, and your child is growing up in it. Speak in the way that comes naturally to you, so your words are warm, clear and confident. If you have questions about which language to focus on for your child, that is a great one to bring to your child's speech therapist.

## Turn daily routines into language moments

You don't need to find extra time. The routines you already do every day are full of chances to practise, because they repeat. Repetition helps words feel familiar.

- **Bath time:** "Paani. Garam paani. Soap. Bubbles! Pop pop pop."
- **Getting dressed:** "Shirt. Arm in. Head in. Shirt on!"
- **Mealtime:** "Roti. More roti? Khatam!"
- **Going out:** "Shoes. Chappal on. Bye bye, ghar!"

Use the same few words each time, in the same order. After a week or two, try pausing before the last word ("Shirt…") and see if your child fills it in ("…on!").

A visual schedule can help here too. When each step of a routine has a picture, you have a natural thing to point to and name together: "First brush. Then bath." Children who aren't speaking much yet can point to the picture, which is communication too.

## Try this today: the "one more word" game

Pick one snack your child loves, maybe a few pieces of banana or a handful of puffed rice. Instead of putting it all in a bowl, keep it with you and hand it over one piece at a time.

Each time, wait a moment for your child to ask, in whatever way they can: a look, a reach, a sound, a word. Then say the word for them, with one extra word added:

- They reach. You say, "Banana," and give it.
- They say, "Nana." You say, "More banana," and give it.
- They say, "More banana." You say, "More banana, please!" and give it, with a big smile.

Keep it short, five minutes at most, and stop while it's still fun. The goal isn't to get a perfect sentence today. It's to show your child that words make good things happen, and that you're listening.

## Be gentle with yourself

Language grows slowly, and it doesn't always grow in a straight line. There may be weeks where a new word appears every day, and weeks where nothing seems to change. Both are normal parts of the journey.

Notice the small things. A new sound. A point instead of a cry. A word used in a new place. Write them down if you can; on the hard days, that list is a good reminder of how far your child has come.
