"Pehle bath, phir cartoon." Most of us have said something like this a hundred times. Sometimes it works. Often, for a child who finds spoken words hard to hold on to, the words float away and all that is left is the feeling of being pulled away from something they love.

A **first-then board** takes that same sentence and puts it in front of your child as two pictures. On the left: what happens first. On the right: what comes next. It is one of the simplest visual supports you can make, and for many families it is the easiest place to begin before moving on to a full daily schedule.

## What a first-then board is (and why pictures help)

A first-then board is a card or strip divided into two boxes, usually labelled **First** and **Then** (or **Pehle** and **Phir**). You place a picture of the less-preferred activity in the first box and a picture of something your child enjoys in the second.

Visual supports like this are well researched. The AFIRM team at the University of North Carolina notes that, based on the 2020 systematic review by the National Clearinghouse on Autism Evidence and Practice (NCAEP), visual supports meet the criteria for an evidence-based practice. Their materials describe a first/then schedule as "a type of visual support that includes the sequencing of two events to help a student understand what comes next" ([AFIRM Visual Supports brief](https://afirm.fpg.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/Visual-Supports-Brief-Packet.pdf)).

Why does this help? A picture stays put. Your words are gone the moment you say them, but the board is still there when your child looks back ten seconds later, wondering, "*Phir kya hoga?*" It answers the question without you repeating yourself.

## When a first-then board is most useful

You don't need one for every moment of the day. It shines in the tricky spots:

- **Transitions away from something fun**, like switching off the TV or leaving the park.
- **Tasks your child resists**, such as brushing teeth, nail cutting, or a hair wash.
- **Short bits of homework or therapy practice**, for example "Pehle 5 words, phir blocks."
- **Outings with a boring middle**, like "Pehle sabzi market, phir ice cream."
- **New or unfamiliar things**, like a first visit to a new doctor or a relative's house.

If your child already uses a daily visual schedule, the first-then board can sit alongside it as a zoomed-in view of "right now, and right after."

## How to make one at home

You can make a first-then board in an evening with things you already have.

### What you need

- A piece of thick card, a file folder, or an A5 sheet you can laminate (or cover with clear packing tape).
- Two boxes drawn side by side, with **First / Pehle** and **Then / Phir** written on top.
- A small set of picture cards: photos from your phone, simple drawings, or printed picture cards.
- Velcro dots or a strip of double-sided tape, so cards can be swapped.

The Vanderbilt Kennedy Center's caregiver tip sheet suggests choosing the visual style that works best for your child, whether that's photos, line drawings or written words, and adding a strip of Velcro on the back to store cards you are not using ([First-Then caregiver tip sheet](https://vkc.vumc.org/assets/files/tipsheets/First_Then_Caregiver.pdf)). If your child reads, a dry-erase board with words works too.

### Choose the right pictures

Start with the activities that come up every day. A good starter set might be:

- **First cards:** brush teeth (*daant saaf*), bath (*nahana*), eat (*khana*), shoes on (*joote pehno*), homework, car seat.
- **Then cards:** TV, phone time, swing, bubbles, a favourite snack, *dadi ke saath khelna*, a song.

Use the words your family actually uses. If your child hears "nahana" at home, put "nahana" on the card, not just "bath." Bilingual labels help everyone in the house, including grandparents and helpers, say the same thing.

If you'd rather not cut and paste, you can make picture cards and a first-then strip on [Visual Schedules](https://visualschedule.app) using bilingual Hindi-English cards, then print it or keep it on your phone for outings.

## How to use it so it actually works

The board is simple. Using it well takes a little practice. Here are the steps that matter most.

### 1. Practise when things are calm

Don't introduce the board in the middle of a meltdown. Try it first with an easy pair, something your child is happy to do: "Pehle juice, phir bubbles." The tip sheet from Vanderbilt recommends a few short practice runs so your child learns how the system works. Once they see the pattern (first thing, then good thing), you can use it for harder moments.

### 2. Keep the "first" small

The first activity should be short and clear. "Pehle homework" is huge and vague. "Pehle 3 sums" is doable. You can always stretch it later. A first-then board builds trust, so set your child up to win.

### 3. Make the "then" something they truly want

The "then" box only works if it holds something your child cares about today, not what *you* think should motivate them. Interests change, so rotate. Vanderbilt's guidance puts it plainly: "Consider changing reinforcers frequently to keep them effective." Offering a choice of two "then" cards before you start can also help: "Swing ya bubbles?"

### 4. Keep your word, right away

This is the most important rule. When the first activity is done, the "then" happens immediately. Not after one more thing. Not "baad mein." If the board says "Pehle shoes, phir park," and then you remember you need to stop at the ATM first, the board loses its meaning. If plans change, change the board in front of your child and show them.

### 5. Use few words

Point to the first card and say, "Pehle brush." When it's done, take the card off, say "Done! Khatam," and point to the next one: "Ab cartoon." Short, calm, same words each time. Let the pictures do the talking.

### 6. Stay where your child can see it

Keep the board at your child's eye level, near where the activity happens: on the bathroom door for brushing, on the dining table for homework, in your bag for outings.

## A few everyday examples

**Morning rush:** "Pehle uniform, phir biscuit." Lay the uniform card next to a picture of their favourite biscuit, and keep the actual biscuit visible (but out of reach).

**Leaving the park:** Before you even leave home, show "Pehle park, phir car, phir khana." For some children, three steps works better than two once they know the idea. That is your sign they may be ready for a longer visual schedule.

**Therapy homework:** If your child's speech or occupational therapist has given you practice at home, ask them how they use first-then in sessions. Using the same words and pictures at home helps practice carry over.

## When it doesn't seem to work

Every child is different, and a first-then board is a tool, not a fix for everything. If it isn't working, look at the basics first: Is the "first" too long? Is the "then" truly motivating today? Has the "then" ever been skipped or delayed? Is your child able to understand the pictures you chose?

If your child is very distressed, or you're unsure what is behind the resistance, talk to your child's therapist or special educator. They can help you adjust the board to your child's needs and stage.

## Start small this week

Pick one tricky moment in your day, just one, and make a first-then card for it. Use it every time for a week. Many parents find that once a child trusts the board, the next step to a morning or bedtime schedule feels much easier.
